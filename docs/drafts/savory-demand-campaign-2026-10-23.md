# How to Review a Logo Proposal Before You Approve It

**Review a logo against the job it needs to do, then decide whether the next step is refinement, a different direction or a clearer brief.**

“It doesn’t feel right yet” can be an honest response to a presentation.

The difficult part is turning that feeling into feedback the designer can act on.

Another round of options may help when the idea is unresolved, but it may also reopen decisions that were already working.

Before you ask for changes, identify what you are approving and what is still uncertain.

## Start with the job of the identity

A useful review begins with the business, the audience and the places the identity will appear.

Write a sentence that describes the job in plain language.

For example: “We need a real-estate team identity that feels assured and personal, reads clearly on our marketing, and fits the way we present ourselves alongside our brokerage.”

That is an illustrative brief, not a quotation from a client.

It gives you something more specific to discuss than “luxury,” “modern” or “professional” on their own.

Ask the designer to explain how the proposed direction answers that job.

The [Design Council’s Framework for Innovation](https://www.designcouncil.org.uk/resources/framework-for-innovation/) separates understanding and defining a problem from developing and testing possible solutions.

The same distinction is useful in a logo review: if you disagree about the problem, changing the lettering may not resolve the disagreement.

## Separate the idea from the execution

Two designs can require very different next steps even when your first reaction to both is “not quite.”

An **execution issue** could be spacing, a detail that disappears at a small size, or a type treatment that needs refinement.

A **direction issue** is more fundamental: the personality feels wrong for the business, the idea depends on a story the audience will never hear, or the identity belongs to a different kind of company.

A **brief issue** appears when the people reviewing the work want incompatible outcomes or have not agreed whom the identity needs to serve.

Try describing the unresolved part before prescribing a fix.

“The name becomes difficult to read when we reduce it for a profile image” gives the designer a concrete problem.

“Make it bolder” is one possible response, but it may not be the best one.

The designer could adjust the lettering, introduce a small-size version or use a separate icon.

**Explain the problem clearly enough to leave room for design judgment.**

## Decide what is worth keeping

A review does not need to treat every part of the identity as provisional.

List the elements that already work and explain why they matter.

That might include a recognizable name, a color customers already associate with the business, or a symbol that is useful in a small space.

Separate those reasons from personal attachment to a detail.

“Customers recognize this mark on our vehicles” is different from “I have always liked this shape.”

Both can be discussed, but they are different kinds of evidence.

If you are changing an identity that already has recognition, my guides to a [brand refresh](https://www.ebaqdesign.com/blog/brand-refresh) and [logo redesign](https://www.ebaqdesign.com/blog/logo-redesign) explore what may be worth preserving.

The goal of the review is a useful identity, not a change for its own sake.

## Test the work in three applications

Start with the places your team needs to use the identity first.

For one business that could be packaging, an ecommerce header and a shipping label.

For a real-estate team it might be a listing presentation, a business card and a social profile.

Choose examples from your own workflow rather than adding impressive mockups without a reason.

**A real application makes the tradeoffs visible.**

You can judge whether the name reads, whether the type hierarchy is clear, and whether the system gives enough flexibility for different content.

Ask to see a small-size version and a single-color version when those are relevant to the intended uses.

A delicate detail that looks excellent at presentation size may need a different treatment when it is reduced.

That does not automatically mean the entire concept is wrong.

### A useful distinction about mockups

Mockups show how an identity could appear in a situation.

They can help you understand scale, contrast and consistency, but they do not establish a sales result.

A billboard image is not evidence that the identity wins more listings, and a social-profile mockup does not prove higher engagement.

Use the presentation to assess the design decisions, then measure business results separately after the work is in use.

## Give feedback that identifies the next decision

Collect your team's responses before sending a list of changes.

Look for the underlying issue when several people describe the same discomfort in different ways.

If one person asks for heavier type and another asks for a darker color, both may be responding to a need for more presence at a small size.

That example is hypothetical, but it shows why combining the observations can be more useful than passing on two separate instructions.

Use this short feedback structure:

- **What we need:** the audience, application or communication goal.
- **What we observe:** the specific part that works or causes a problem.
- **Why it matters:** the effect on the intended use.
- **What needs a decision:** the question you want the next round to resolve.
- **Who approves it:** the person responsible for consolidating the team's response.

For example: “On the mobile profile, the full name is difficult to read; we need people to recognize the team at that size, so please show the recommended small-size treatment.”

This gives the designer a clear test while leaving room to propose the right response.

## Agree who is deciding

Feedback becomes difficult when every person can reopen an approved decision without a shared reason.

Identify the final decision-maker and the people who need to contribute before the next presentation.

Ask reviewers to connect a comment to the brief, the audience or a practical use.

Then consolidate the response so the designer receives a coherent set of priorities.

The [AIGA proposal guide](https://designcareers.aiga.org/career-resources/freelance-graphic-design-10/freelance-graphic-design-proposal-guide-2026-116) also emphasizes defining responsibilities, review stages and handoff expectations in the working scope.

A clear review process helps everyone understand what is being decided and what happens after approval.

## Choose the next step explicitly

Use the review to agree the next piece of work.

| What the review reveals | Useful next step |
|---|---|
| The concept fits, but a specific detail is not working | Refine that detail and test it in the intended use |
| The team agrees on the goal, but the concept does not express it | Explore a different direction against the same brief |
| The reviewers want different audiences or personalities | Resolve the brief and approval priorities first |
| The logo is approved, but applications are inconsistent | Extend and document the identity system |

**Write down which decision is closed and what the next round must resolve.**

That gives the review a stopping point and helps you avoid treating every presentation as a fresh start.

## Check the handoff before approval

Ask what your team will receive and how those deliverables support the agreed uses.

Depending on the project, that could include vector and image files, logo variations, a color palette, typography and usage guidelines.

Confirm how source files and any third-party fonts or other licensed assets are handled in the agreement.

Then identify the applications that are included now and the ones that need a separate scope.

My [style-guide article](https://www.ebaqdesign.com/blog/style-guides) explains how guidelines help a team continue the identity consistently.

The important distinction is between **approving the direction** and **having everything required for rollout**.

Those milestones can involve different amounts of work.
